[p017] (att2 p.1) X. Conclusion — The FDA comment period that closes on October 19, 2026, and the CMS ACCESS expansion that takes new tracks live on April 1, 2027, are not separate stories. One will decide how generative tools are allowed to speak in clinic. The other will decide whether those tools are worth paying for when the patient has heart failure, COPD, diabetes, pain, depression, or a substance use disorder. Guidelines should start where this essay started: with simple principles. Licensed judgment remains the accountable act. Intended use should be honest about autonomy. Risk is autonomy multiplied by harm. The deployed system, not the model card, is what must be competent. Human review must be designed. Equity is safety. Patients should knowwhen a machine is in the room. Payment should follow work and outcome. Nursing should keep its own language. The professions should govern their tools.
[p018] (att2 p.1) From those principles follow practical models. Nursing assessment is the first safe harbor for clinical AI. Physician assessment remains decision-making, not transcription. Robotic and autonomous diagnosis may be charged only inside a defined code, a defined AppendixS level, and a defined liability structure. CPT and clinical practice guidelines are the twin rails that keep generators from inventing unsafe care. An Al-generated nursing care plan can be a high-quality draft if it is bound to diagnosis, vital signs, and presentation, written in NANDA-NIC-NOC, and authenticated by a nurse. An AI Nursing Association, or an equivalent chartered body, can keep that settlement alive as the tools change.
[p019] (att2 p.1) The assistance offered here is not a claim that the technology is ready to practice nursing or medicine. It is a claim that the technology is already in the workflow, that two federal agencies are now writing the tests it will have to pass, and that the safest next step is to write those tests in public, in plain language, before the tools write them for us.

[p040] (att4 p.1) E. Placement on the FDA risk axes — An AI nursing care-plan generator, used as described, is informational and at most actiondirecting. It does not take autonomous clinical action. Consequence of an incorrect draft is real— missed deterioration, wasted effort, or a misleading problem list—but it is mitigated if authentication is mandatory and if high-consequence suggestions (sepsis concern, suicidal ideation, severe withdrawal, acute decompensation) force an interruptive escalation rather than a quiet line in a plan. Moving the same generator into unsupervised order entry or autonomous diagnosis would change the cell and should change the regulatory and payment treatment.

[p047] (att5 p.1) A. Inputs — The generator should require three input classes and refuse to emit a plan if any class is empty. [p053] (att6 p.1) C. Liability is not solved by a code — A billable autonomous output does not answer who is responsible when the output is wrong. Manufacturer duty runs to design, validation, labeling, and change control. Facility duty runs to selection, integration, privilege, and monitoring. Clinician duty runs to appropriate use, contestability, and the decision actually implemented. Insurers have not uniformly confirmed that professional liability policies cover algorithm -initiated management. Guidelines should require that any organization billing for autonomous diagnostic outputs maintain written allocation of these duties, indemnification terms that do not leave the bedside clinician as the sole residual payer, and a patient-facing explanation of what the machine did.

[p065] (att7 p.1) V. Diagnosis with Robots and Machines: Autonomy, Charges, and Liability — A. Three levels of machine diagnosis
[p066] (att7 p.1) The AMA CPT Appendix S taxonomy, revised in 2026, is the most useful existing language for charges. Assistive software supports clinician work with data described as likelihood, suggestive of, or risk for; a physician or other qualified health professional must interpret and report the output. Augmentative software produces clinically meaningful parameters that differ from the input and identify a condition in predictive or prognostic terms; documentation must show that the output contributed to management. Autonomous software derives parameters and independently generates clinically meaningful interpretations. Autonomous work is further divided into three levels. Level I recommends a diagnosis or management option that remains contestable and requires clinician action to implement. Level II initiates diagnosis or management with an alert and an opportunity to override. Level III initiates management that proceeds unless the clinician actively contests it.
[p067] (att7 p.1) The FDA two-axis framework maps onto this taxonomy with unusual cleanliness. Assistive and most augmentative tools sit toward informational and action-directing activity. Autonomous Level II and Level III tools sit at supervised or fully autonomous action. Consequence determines how much evidence and monitoring each cell requires. A robotic kiosk that tells a well adult their cardiovascular risk score is not the same product as a closed-loop robot that initiates insulin, ora generative system that issues a billable diagnosis of heart failure from wearable data without concurrent clinician involvement.
[p068] (att7 p.1) B. Possible charges for machine-assisted and autonomous diagnoses — Charges should follow existing constructs before anyone invents a generic “AI diagnosis” code. Where the clinician performs and documents the assessment, evaluation-and-management codes, chronic-care management, principal-care management, and ACCESS outcome-aligned payments remain the backbone. Wherea device supplies physiologic data, remote physiologic monitoring codes apply, including the 2026 additions that recognize shorter windows: CPT 99445 for device supply with two to fifteen days of data in a thirty-day period, and CPT 99470 for ten to nineteen minutes of treatment management. Traditional 99453, 99454, 99457, and 99458 remain available for setup, sixteen-to-thirty-day supply, and twenty-minute management increments. Remote therapeutic monitoring codes follow a parallel structure for musculoskeletal and respiratory programs relevant to ACCESS tracks.

[p070] (att8 p.1) Guideline Contribution - Generative Al in Clinical Assessment and Chronic Care (ADPIE)—gives AI a bounded job: assemble subjective and objective data, flag missing elements, propose standardized nursing diagnoses, and draft a plan for a licensed nurse to accept or revise.
[p071] (att8 p.1) A. What the machine may do — An AI assessment layer may ingest vital signs, device streams, chief concern, history, medications, allergies, functional status, and presentation. It may organize those data into review-of-systems and focused-assessment formats. It may compare current vital signs against the patient’s baseline and against condition-specific thresholds relevant to ACCESS tracks— blood pressure and weight in heart failure, oxygen saturation and respiratory rate in COPD, pain scores in musculoskeletal disease, and validated symptom scales in depression, anxiety, or substance use disorder. It may propose NANDA-I labels with related factors and defining characteristics, ranked by priority. It may draft SMART outcomes using Nursing Outcomes Classification language and suggest Nursing Interventions Classification actions tied to current clinical practice guidelines.
[p072] (att8 p.1) B. What the machine may not do — The machine may not complete the legal nursing assessment. It may not assign a medical diagnosis. It may not silently drop a finding that does not fit its template. It may not treat a generated care plan as implemented until a nurse reviews, individualizes, and authenticates it. It may not replace the interpersonal work through which nurses detect fear, confusion, cultural meaning, and the small physical changes that do not yet appear in a data stream. ANA’s 2026 Think Tank consensus is direct on this point: AI must support, never replace, nursing judgment; human oversight is non-negotiable; black-box systems are incompatible with professional accountability; and Al literacy is now baseline competence.
[p073] (att8 p.1) C. A simple nursing-assessment architecture — A guideline-ready architecture has five layers. Capture: devices and conversation produce structured and unstructured data, with provenance. Interpretation: models map data to assessment domains and to standardized nursing language. Proposal: the system presents ranked nursing diagnoses, outcomes, and interventions, each with a confidence statement and source links to the triggering data and to a CPG or institutional protocol. Authentication: the nurse accepts, edits, or rejects, and the record stores the human act. Learning: de-identified accepted and rejected proposals feed local quality review, not silent model self-training on identified patient data without governance.

[p078] (att9 p.1) Guideline Contribution - Generative Al in Clinical Assessment and Chronic Care ll. Foundational Principles: How Al Can Be in Healthcare
[p079] (att9 p.1) The principles below are intentionally simple. Complexity belongs in implementation, not in first principles. They are consistent with the American Nurses Association’s position that AI must support and not replace nursing judgment; with the American Academy of Nursing’s 2026 statement that AI should support rather than supplant human wisdom; with the AMA CPT Appendix S taxonomy that separates assistive, augmentative, and autonomous software; and with the FDA’s emerging two-axis view of activity and consequence.
[p080] (att9 p.1) 1. Licensed judgment remains the accountable act. Software may draft, retrieve, score, or recommend. A nurse, physician, or other qualified health professional remains responsible for the assessment, diagnosis, plan, and order that enter the legal record.
[p081] (att9 p.1) 2. Intended use must be stated at the lowest defensible level of autonomy. A documentation assistant should not be labeled or marketed as a diagnostician. A care-plan generator should not silently become an order-entry agent.
[p082] (att9 p.1) 3. Risk is a product of autonomy and harm, not of marketing language. Informational, reversible, and easily contested outputs require less premarket and postmarket burden than action-directing or autonomous outputs that can delay emergency care, initiate treatment, or generate a billable diagnosis.
[p083] (att9 p.1) 4. The unit of evaluation is the deployed system. Foundation models, prompts, retrieval stores, guardrails, and user interface together constitute the device or clinical tool. Benchmarking a model in isolation is not competency assessment of the product patients will meet.
[p084] (att9 p.1) 5. Human review is not a slogan. It is a designed control: contestability, override, escalation, and an audit trail that shows who accepted, edited, or rejected an output and why.
[p085] (att9 p.1) 6. Equity and representativeness are safety requirements. Performance must be examined across age, sex, race and ethnicity where clinically relevant, language, disability, and care setting, because biased outputs are clinical errors.
[p086] (att9 p.1) 7. Transparency to the patient is part of consent. When AI is used in diagnosis, triage, treatment planning, documentation, or decision support, that use should be disclosed in language the patient can understand.
[p087] (att9 p.1) 8. Payment must follow work and outcome, not the mere presence of an algorithm. A charge for an Al-generated diagnosis is legitimate only when the service meets a defined CPT or HCPCS construct, a qualified professional remains accountable at the appropriate Appendix S level, and medical necessity is documented.
[p088] (att9 p.1) 9. Nursing knowledge has its own formal language. Care plans generated by machines should speak NANDA-I, NIC, and NOC, or an equally standardized local taxonomy, so that nursing work remains measurable rather than absorbed into a physician note.
[p089] (att9 p.1) 10. The professions must govern their own tools. Medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and allied fields need standing organizations that can set competencies, comment on dockets, maintain practice standards, and prevent vendor capture of clinical language.

[p094] (att10 p.1) B. The CMS ACCESS expansion: judging the outcome — ACCESS is a ten-year CMS Innovation Center model that began July 5, 2026, and is scheduled to run through June 30, 2036. It tests outcome-aligned payments for technology-supported chronic care in Original Medicare. Participating organizations receive predictable payments for managing qualifying conditions and earn the full amount only when patients meet defined health goals. The initial tracks address early cardio-kidney-metabolic conditions, established cardio-kidney-metabolic disease, chronic musculoskeletal pain, and behavioral health. The September 2026 expansion adds heart failure, COPD, substance use disorder, and tobacco cessation, plus a follow-on musculoskeletal track, with new tracks starting April 1, 2027. CMS estimates that three of four people with Medicare now qualify for at least one track. Approximately 160 organizations were participating at launch, with rolling applications continuing through 2033.
[p095] (att10 p.1) ACCESS is not a device-authorization pathway. It is a payment and accountability pathway. CMS has stated that services may include clinician consultations, lifestyle and behavioral support, medication management, care coordination, and use of FDA-cleared hardware and software, including Al-enabled technologies and connected devices. Primary care clinicians may bill a comanagement payment, approximately thirty dollars subject to geographic adjustment, for reviewing ACCESS care updates and documentinga related coordination action. The model therefore creates demand for AI documentation, remote monitoring, and care-planning tools— and simultaneously creates a performance standard against which those tools will be judged: did blood pressure, symptoms, function, or recovery actually improve?
[p096] (att10 p.1) C. The joint test for Al documentation and chronic-care tools — A tool that drafts notes, proposes nursing diagnoses, or triages remote vital signs will now face two audiences at once. FDA will ask whether the function is informational or action-taking, how severe an error would be, whether competency has been shown on the deployed system, and how drift, hallucination, and foundation-model change will be monitored. CMS, through ACCESS and related remote-care payment policy, will ask whether the same tool supports measurable chronic-care outcomes without inflating claims or displacing the referring clinician. Guidelines that ignore either audience will be incomplete. Guidelines that begin with simple principles, then map those principles onto assessment, diagnosis, coding, and professional organization, can serve both.

[p101] (att11 p.1) Comment deadline: October 19, 2026 - Prepared September 24, 2026 — Abstract — Two federal actions in 2026 are jointly defining how artificial intelligence will be judged in American healthcare. On August 18, 2026, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a discussion paper on the regulation of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)—enabled medical devices and opened docket FDA-2026-N-7874 for public comment through October 19, 2026. On September 15, 2026, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) expanded the Advancing Chronic Care with Effective, Scalable Solutions (ACCESS) Model, adding heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, substance use disorder, and tobacco cessation tracks beginning April 1, 2027. Together, these moves establish a dual test: devices and documentation tools will be judged first by safety, competency, and lifecycle risk, and second by whether they produce measurable improvement in chronic conditions under outcome-aligned payment.
[p102] (att11 p.1) This essay offers assistance in developing guidelines. It begins with simple foundational principles. It then specifies how those principles apply to nursing assessment, physician assessment, robotic and autonomous diagnosis (including charges), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) classification, clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) as an evidence layer, anda practical model for Al-generated nursing care plans based on diagnosis, vital signs, and presentation. It concludes with a model for an AI Nursing Association capable of developing and maintaining the profession’s growth into Al-enabled care. The governing idea is modest and firm: artificial intelligence may inform, organize, and scale clinical work; it may not replace licensed judgment, patient consent, or accountable human review.

The paper is explicit that it does not establish policy, does not set evidentiary requirements for future submissions, and does not resolve whether the approaches discussed fall within existing legal authorities. Its purpose is to solicit early input on four clusters of questions: risk assessment, premarket evaluation, postmarket monitoring, and issues specific to foundation models and agentic systems.
