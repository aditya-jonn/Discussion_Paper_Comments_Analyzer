[p001] (body) I reviewed the FDA discussion paper Discussion Paper Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices. Overall, I think this is one of the most thoughtful FDA discussion papers published to date on GenAI regulation and it aligns well with a Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC) approach. It appropriately recognizes that traditional software validation approaches are insufficient for open-ended GenAI systems and introduces a risk-based + competency-based framework. However, As someone working on cloud-based medical software platforms, there are the areas where I believe additional inputs you can consider before making any final paper/draft guideline/Recommendations. Pl find the enclosed document for Suggestions.
[p002] (att1 p.1) Review comments By Jagadeesha: 1. Missing Explicit Multiple Function Device Product (MFDP) Considerations
[p003] (att1 p.1) Although FDA briefly references multiple-function products, the discussion does not sufficiently address: • Medical + non-medical GenAI functions in the same platform
[p004] (att1 p.1) • Shared foundation models servicing both regulated and non-regulated functions — • Contamination or influence between regulated and non-regulated outputs
[p005] (att1 p.1) • Segregation requirements — • Architecture Recommendations inclusion Medical + non-medical GenAI functions
[p006] (att1 p.1) This is particularly important for platforms like where some functions could be medicaldevice functions while others remain non-device administrative functions.
[p007] (att1 p.1) Recommendation — FDA should provide: • Explicit MFDP examples — • Boundary control expectations
[p008] (att1 p.1) • Evidence requirements demonstrating independence between regulated and nonregulated functions — 2. Cybersecurity is Underrepresented
[p009] (att1 p.1) The paper references guardrails and prompt injection but cybersecurity requirements are not sufficiently integrated into the framework.
[p010] (att1 p.1) Particularly absent: • Model poisoning — • Retrieval database attacks — • Prompt injection impact on clinical outputs
[p011] (att1 p.1) • Adversarial attacks — • Agentic tool abuse — • LLM supply chain security
[p012] (att1 p.1) Recommendation — Add a dedicated cybersecurity competency area or explicitly align with: • FDA Cybersecurity Guidance
[p013] (att1 p.1) • SBOM expectations — • IEC 81001-5-1 — • NIST AI RMF — 3. Human Factors / Usability Not Prominent Enough
[p014] (att1 p.2) The paper discusses communication quality and automation bias. However, it does not explicitly connect them to traditional human factors engineering requirements.
[p015] (att1 p.2) For GenAI systems FDA should evaluate: • User trust calibration — • Overreliance
[p016] (att1 p.2) • Alert fatigue — • Automation bias — • Explainability effectiveness — Recommendation
[p017] (att1 p.2) Human factors validation should become a standalone evaluation pillar.
[p018] (att1 p.2) 4. Lack of Clear Treatment for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) — Most healthcare GenAI systems will not rely solely on foundation models.
[p019] (att1 p.2) Instead, they will rely upon: • RAG architectures — • Clinical knowledge repositories
[p020] (att1 p.2) • Guideline retrieval systems — • Enterprise databases — The discussion focuses heavily on foundation models but insufficiently addresses:
[p021] (att1 p.2) • Retrieval quality — • Source governance — • Knowledge base drift — • Citation accuracy
[p022] (att1 p.2) Recommendation — FDA should define validation expectations separately for: • Foundation model — • Retrieval layer
[p023] (att1 p.2) • Orchestrator layer — • Final clinical output — 5. Statistics and Acceptance Criteria Remain Vague
[p024] (att1 p.3) One major weakness is that the document does not provide practical approaches for:
[p025] (att1 p.3) • Sample size determination — • Confidence intervals — • Performance thresholds — • Statistical significance for open-ended outputs
[p026] (att1 p.3) FDA raises the question but does not suggest a path forward.
[p027] (att1 p.3) Recommendation — Consider: • Non-inferiority approaches — • Bayesian approaches — • Risk-adjusted performance thresholds
[p028] (att1 p.3) • Human-AI team effectiveness measures — 6. Agentic AI Requires Stronger Controls — Agentic AI is discussed but I think the risk is greater than portrayed.
[p029] (att1 p.3) Agentic systems can: • Execute actions — • Trigger workflows — • Modify records
[p030] (att1 p.3) • Interact with connected systems — Single-step validation is insufficient.
[p031] (att1 p.3) Recommendation — Require: • Action approval checkpoints — • Tool-use verification — • Audit trails
[p032] (att1 p.3) • Rollback capabilities — • Runtime supervision before high-risk autonomous deployment.
[p033] (att1 p.3) 7. Third-Party Foundation Model Change Management — This is probably the biggest practical regulatory challenge.
[p034] (att1 p.4) The paper recognises that foundation model providers may change their models without sponsors initiating updates.
[p035] (att1 p.4) For medical device manufacturers, this creates a major regulatory problem because: • Behaviour may change overnight
[p036] (att1 p.4) • Performance may drift — • Safety profiles may change — • FDA-cleared evidence may no longer reflect deployed behaviour
[p037] (att1 p.4) Recommendation — FDA should strongly consider: • Locked model versions — • Mandatory model version traceability
[p038] (att1 p.4) • Supplier quality agreements — • Triggered revalidation criteria — • PCCP-specific provisions for foundation-model updates
